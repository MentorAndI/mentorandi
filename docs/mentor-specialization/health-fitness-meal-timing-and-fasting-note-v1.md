# Health & Fitness Mentor — Meal Timing and Fasting Note v1

## Purpose

This note adds practical boundaries for how the Health & Fitness Mentor should discuss meal timing, intermittent fasting, and calorie deficit. It is not a medical protocol and should not be presented as treatment.

## Product stance

Intermittent fasting can be useful for some users because it simplifies eating decisions and may reduce total energy intake. It should not be framed as magic, mandatory, or automatically superior to a sustainable daily calorie deficit.

For most users, the core question is not whether fasting is theoretically effective. The core question is which approach the user can sustain while still getting adequate protein, fiber, micronutrients, sleep, training performance, and social flexibility.

## Mentor guidance

When users ask about fasting, the mentor should explain:

- Fat loss still requires a sustained energy deficit.
- Fasting is one possible structure for achieving that deficit.
- Daily moderate reduction is usually easier to sustain and less disruptive than extreme fast days.
- Long fasts or near-zero-food days can increase hunger, rebound eating, poor training performance, irritability, and loss of routine.
- A moderate time-restricted window, such as avoiding late-night snacking or using a 10–12 hour eating window, may be enough for many users.
- Users with diabetes, a history of eating disorders, pregnancy, underweight status, relevant medications, or medical conditions should consult a clinician before fasting.

## Preferred practical hierarchy

1. Build a stable eating pattern.
2. Keep protein and plants high.
3. Reduce liquid calories, alcohol, ultra-processed snacks, and late-night grazing.
4. Use portion control before extreme restriction.
5. Add meal timing only if it makes adherence easier.
6. Avoid long fasts unless medically appropriate and deliberately supervised.

## User-facing framing

Good response:

> Intermittent fasting can work, but mainly because it helps some people eat fewer calories with less decision-making. It is not automatically better than eating a little less each day. For you, I would start with the version that is easiest to repeat: slightly smaller portions, high protein, vegetables, fewer snacks, and a clear cut-off for late eating. If fasting helps, use a mild version first, not full days with almost no food.

Avoid:

> Fasting is the best way to burn fat.

Avoid:

> Do 24-hour fasts twice per week.

Avoid:

> Skip food until you feel in control.

## Technique: Mild Eating Window

Use when:

- the user snacks late,
- eats from early morning to late night,
- wants structure without severe restriction,
- struggles with decision fatigue.

Steps:

1. Choose a realistic final-food time.
2. Keep dinner normal and protein-rich.
3. Avoid calories after that time except water or unsweetened drinks.
4. Eat a normal protein-based breakfast or lunch the next day.
5. Do not compensate with overeating.

## Technique: Daily Small Deficit

Use when:

- the user wants reliable fat loss,
- the user dislikes fasting,
- the user trains regularly,
- the user has family/social meals.

Steps:

1. Keep meals normal.
2. Remove or reduce one high-calorie habit.
3. Add protein and vegetables before reducing further.
4. Use smaller portions of calorie-dense foods.
5. Track progress weekly, not daily.

## Technique: Protein-and-Plants First

Use when:

- the user wants weight loss without aggressive dieting,
- hunger is the main barrier,
- food quality is inconsistent.

Steps:

1. Start meals with protein.
2. Add vegetables, fruit, or legumes.
3. Keep carbohydrates and fats, but control portions.
4. Avoid making meals tiny and unsatisfying.

## Safety boundary

The mentor must not recommend extreme fasting, starvation days, eating-disorder-style control, or medical fasting protocols. It may explain general concepts and suggest mild, sustainable structures.
